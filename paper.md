title: 'Probing gray matter microstructure differences in neurodegenerative dementia using b-tensor encoding diffusion MRI'
abstract: Dementia is a major health concern in the aging population, with the number of people living with dementia expected to increase. Quantitative MRI techniques can offer information about microstructural changes in the brain, making them valuable tools to study and diagnose these diseases. This work studied the sensitivity of advanced diffusion MRI metrics obtained from b-tensor encoding (microscopic fractional anisotropy; isotropic kurtosis) to the microstructural changes of Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and Lewy body dementia. We complemented diffusion MRI with volumetric assessments and magnetization transfer MRI, which is sensitive to myelin, and focused on multiple gray matter regions known to be affected by these diseases. We performed an additional analysis in the hippocampus at the subfield level, given its central role in Alzheimer’s disease, where certain subfields are known to be more strongly affected than others. We showed that the multidimensional space spanned by these metrics has distinct patterns for Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia compared to healthy controls, with microscopic fractional anisotropy contributing to differences between groups, particularly in the hippocampus. Accordingly, microscopic fractional anisotropy has the potential to complement other imaging markers of neurodegeneration in the study of dementia.

# Introduction

Dementia is a major health concern in the aging population, and the number of people affected is expected to increase [@breijyeh_comprehensive_2020]. In the early stages of dementia, cellular changes in the brain precede volumetric losses and the manifestation of cognitive decline [@weston_diffusion_2015;@jack_hypothetical_2010]. Different dementias, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and Lewy body dementia (LBD), show spatially different patterns of atrophy, the underpinnings of which are not fully understood. Post-mortem brain histological studies have revealed hallmark cellular microstructural alterations of these disorders, such as amyloid-β plaque deposition and neurofibrillary tangle accumulation accompanied by neuronal and synaptic loss in AD, neuronal loss and gliosis associated with tau or TDP-43 inclusions in FTD, and Lewy bodies accompanied by neuronal and synaptic loss in LBD [@serrano-pozo_neuropathological_2011;@mackenzie_molecular_2016;@sekiya_neuropathology_2025;@zetterberg_biofluid_2026]. Despite their distinct molecular underpinnings, these disorders share several microstructural neurodegenerative changes: neuronal and neurite loss, synaptic degeneration, and reactive astrogliosis/microgliosis. Accordingly, the in vivo quantification of these alterations, particularly using sensitive neuroimaging biomarkers, could help with earlier diagnosis or support other dementia biomarkers [@zetterberg_biofluid_2026]. Moreover, the clinical presentations of these dementias often overlap, and knowledge of the spatial distribution of neurodegeneration and of the differences between dementias, especially in the earlier stages, could provide insight into microstructural alterations before cognitive decline.

Diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI) [@le_bihan_mr_1986] is a technology sensitive to microstructural cellular properties. dMRI indirectly infers cellular information by measuring the random molecular motion of water, which is hindered by cellular membranes [@beaulieu_basis_2002]. It is highly sensitive to microstructural alterations produced by neurodegeneration [@afzali_sensitivity_2021;@concha_macroscopic_2014], and is widely applied clinically to assess stroke and detect tumors [@drake-perez_clinical_2018]. The majority of dMRI research has used diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) [@basser_estimation_1994] to quantify microstructural changes. DTI provides metrics such as mean diffusivity (MD), which quantifies average diffusivity, and fractional anisotropy (FA), which quantifies the degree of directional water diffusion per voxel. An MD increase could be explained by a general loss of microstructure, while FA increases with the coherence of aligned axon bundles. In AD [@chen_abnormal_2023;@acosta-cabronero_diffusion_2014;@ashford_diffusion_2011;@naggara_diffusion_2006;@weston_diffusion_2015], FTD [@gordon_advances_2016;@mahoney_longitudinal_2015] and LBD [@laurell_systematic_2025], observations typically include MD increases across gray and white matter, and FA decreases in white matter (suggesting degradation of axon tracts). dMRI studies comparing dementia subtypes have identified overlapping but also disease-specific white matter tract signatures [@tian_advanced_2026]. FTD typically affects anterior tracts, while AD shows a posterior and temporal pattern [@zhang_white_2009;@elahi_longitudinal_2017]. AD has also shown more widespread cortical diffusivity increases than LBD, while LBD has shown distinct amygdala and inferior longitudinal fasciculus changes [@kantarci_dementia_2010]. However, the intrinsic limitations of DTI metrics make them unreliable in regions of dispersed cellular orientation [@novikov_quantifying_2019;@karat_diffusion_2024], which makes it difficult to identify the specific cellular origin of the reported changes. For example, FA is reduced where axon bundles have low orientation coherence, regardless of tissue health, such as in regions with crossing white matter fibers. Similarly, FA is limited for studying gray matter structures where fiber microstructure (e.g., axons and dendrites) is arranged in complex ways or in multiple directions within a voxel.

Recent developments in dMRI have yielded the “b-tensor encoding” acquisition [@westin_q-space_2016]. These acquisitions collect complementary diffusion-weighted scans that encode along multiple directions simultaneously (e.g., “planar” or “spherical tensor encoding”) in addition to the conventional approach of sensitizing the signal to diffusion along a single direction at a time (“linear tensor encoding”). This broader diffusion encoding space can be used to calculate metrics that overcome some of the limitations of DTI metrics; for example, microscopic fractional anisotropy (µFA) quantifies cellular anisotropy irrespective of the sub-voxel orientation coherence (unlike FA) [@lasic_microanisotropy_2014;@westin_q-space_2016;@arezza_rapid_2021;@shemesh_conventions_2016], and isotropic kurtosis (Ki) quantifies the variance of the compartment-specific diffusivities in a voxel, which may correlate with cell size heterogeneity [@szczepankiewicz_link_2016]. µFA and Ki provide additional microstructure information that may be better suited to quantify gray matter [@arezza_hippocampal_2024;@yoo_high-resolution_2022] and cortical regions [@lampinen_tensor-valued_2020]. To date, b-tensor encoding has been used to study white matter microstructural changes in stroke [@zhou_tensor-valued_2024] and multiple sclerosis [@andersen_disentangling_2020], hippocampal and cortical changes in epilepsy [@arezza_hippocampal_2024;@lampinen_tensor-valued_2020], and brain tumors [@szczepankiewicz_link_2016;@nilsson_tensor-valued_2020]. One study found that µFA reductions in the frontal cortex and the splenium of the corpus callosum correlate with increased severity of motor impairment in Parkinson's disease [@kamiya_brain_2020]. However, no studies have examined µFA and Ki in the common neurodegenerative dementias, including AD, FTD, and LBD, or compared them across these dementias [@afzali_quantification_2022;@syed_nasser_review_2022].

This preliminary study investigated advanced dMRI metrics (µFA, Ki) in conjunction with standard dMRI metrics (FA, MD) and volumetric measures in brain regions that are commonly affected across AD, FTD, and LBD, including the frontal, parietal, temporal, cingulate, occipital and insular cortices, the amygdala and the hippocampus [@naggara_diffusion_2006;@finger_frontotemporal_2016;@gordon_advances_2016;@laurell_systematic_2025;@karat_hippocampal_2025;@wearn_quantitative_2025]. To complement the dMRI metrics, we also acquired magnetization transfer saturation (MTsat) [@helms_high-resolution_2008], which is sensitive to myelin content [@seiberlich_wood_chapter_2020] and has been reported to decrease in dementia [@moallemian_multimodal_2023;@wearn_quantitative_2025]. We focused on AD, FTD, and LBD, as they are the most common forms of dementia resulting from primary neurodegenerative cascades. We hypothesized that these metrics would show distinct regional profiles for each dementia, with the greatest reductions in µFA in regions affected earliest in each neurodegenerative dementia, including the parietal lobes and hippocampus, in particular the subiculum and cornu ammonis field 1 (CA1) in AD, the amygdala and occipital regions in LBD, and the frontoinsular and anterior cingulate regions in FTD.

# Methods
## Participants
From December 2020 to July 2025, the Cognitive Neurology and Alzheimer Research Centre at Parkwood Hospital in London, Ontario, Canada, recruited participants with neurodegenerative dementia and age-matched healthy controls (HC) into the HYADES study. Participants with neurodegenerative dementias included those with AD, FTD, or LBD.

Western University’s Health Sciences Research Ethics Board approved the study procedures. Participants, or their substitute decision makers, and their study partners (applicable only to participants with neurodegenerative dementia), provided written informed consent prior to undertaking study procedures.

Inclusion criteria for participants with neurodegenerative dementias included 1) age 30 to 90 years and 2) a clinical diagnosis of dementia based on the relevant diagnostic criteria for AD [@dekosky_revision_2011], FTD [@gorno-tempini_classification_2011;@rascovsky_sensitivity_2011], and LBD [@mckeith_diagnosis_2017]; all participants in the LBD group met the consensus criteria for dementia with Lewy bodies, and participants with Parkinson's disease dementia were not included. Exclusion criteria were 1) history of significant brain tumor, other neurological diseases affecting cognition, or mixed dementias, 2) cognitive or language impairment that prevented the participant from understanding the nature of the study or the study task instructions, and 3) lack of a study partner or caregiver available to provide collateral history regarding the participant’s general functioning and symptoms.

Inclusion criteria for HC participants included 1) age 30 to 90 years, 2) no reported persistent decline in cognitive capacity compared with their usual baseline, and 3) a score on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) of 24 or greater. Exclusion criteria were a history of a significant brain tumor, or neurological or psychiatric disease judged by the investigators to have affected cognition, including FTD, AD, LBD, or related disorders.

## MRI
### Acquisition protocol
The MRI acquisition was performed on a Siemens Prisma 3 T scanner with a 32-channel head coil at the Centre for Functional and Metabolic Mapping at Western University in London, ON, Canada. The protocol consisted of two anatomical acquisitions, a three-part acquisition for MTsat, and a b-tensor encoding dMRI acquisition. 

The anatomical acquisitions included a T1-weighted (T1w) image acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence (TR = 2400 ms, TE = 2.28 ms, flip angle = 8°, acquisition time = 6 min 38 s) and a T2-weighted (T2w) sequence (TR = 3200 ms, TE = 564 ms, acquisition time = 5 min 57 s). Both scans had a field of view (FOV) of 240 × 256 mm{sup}`2`, 0.8 mm isotropic voxel size, and generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions (GRAPPA) with an acceleration factor of 2.

The MTsat protocol included three fast low angle shot (FLASH) 3D acquisitions that had a combined scan time of 5 min 40 s: a proton density-weighted (TR = 360 ms, flip angle = 10°), a magnetization transfer-weighted (TR = 360 ms, flip angle = 10°, with the default vendor radiofrequency pulse for magnetization transfer weighting), and a T1-weighted acquisition (TR = 12 ms, flip angle = 20°). All three scans shared TE = 3.86 ms, FOV = 192 × 256 × 192 mm{sup}`3`, 2 mm isotropic voxel size, partial Fourier of 0.75, and a GRAPPA factor of 2.

The b-tensor dMRI protocol used a multiband echo-planar imaging sequence with an in-house modified diffusion gradient scheme [@arezza_rapid_2021]. The diffusion gradient scheme consisted of linear tensor encoding acquisitions with 15 and 30 directions at b = 1 and 2 ms/µm{sup}`2`, respectively, and 18 and 48 spherical tensor-encoded averages at b = 1 and 2 ms/µm{sup}`2`, respectively. The linear and spherical tensor encoding acquisitions were interleaved with each other within one integrated scan. The protocol also included four b{sub}`0` acquisitions and an additional b{sub}`0` acquisition with reverse-phase encoding for field map correction. Supplementary [](#fig_waveform) shows schematics of the diffusion waveform. The other parameters were TR = 4900 ms, TE = 92 ms, FOV = 229 × 229 mm{sup}`2`, 1.8 mm isotropic voxel size, partial Fourier of 0.75, multiband factor 2, GRAPPA factor 2, and total acquisition time of 11 min 50 s.

### Image pre-processing
We preprocessed dMRI data using the DESIGNER pipeline (version 2.0.04) [@ades-aron_evaluation_2018;@chen_optimization_2024] with the following options: MP-PCA denoising [@veraart_denoising_2016], Rician bias correction [@koay_analytically_2006], partial Fourier Gibbs ringing removal [@lee_removal_2021], and eddy for motion and field map correction [@andersson_integrated_2016;@tournier_mrtrix3_2019]. Note that DESIGNER runs eddy independently for each b-tensor encoding shape (linear and spherical encoding).

We used SynthStrip (in FreeSurfer version 8.1.0) [@hoopes_synthstrip_2022;@fischl_freesurfer_2012] for automatic brain extraction of all images and greedy (version 1.3.0, https://github.com/pyushkevich/greedy) for image registration. The anatomical T1w image served as the participant-level reference target for all co-registrations. For dMRI data, we registered the average b{sub}`0` image to the T2w image, and the T2w image to the reference T1w image. We concatenated these two transformations to align all dMRI-derived metric maps directly to the T1w image in a single interpolation step. We co-registered the MTsat data directly to the native T1w reference space.

For whole-brain segmentation, we created a hybrid segmentation from the outputs of SIAM [@valabregue_siam_2026] and SynthSeg+ (in FreeSurfer version 8.1.0) [@fischl_freesurfer_2012;@billot_robust_2023], both applied to the raw T1w images. Although SIAM provided the most accurate overall segmentation in this cohort, it provides only a single label for total gray matter. To parcel this region further, we combined SIAM's gray matter label with SynthSeg+’s cortical parcellation using the following algorithm: First, we relabeled all SIAM gray matter voxels that overlapped with SynthSeg+ segmentation with their corresponding SynthSeg+ label. Second, for the remaining non-overlapping voxels, we propagated the closest new label until all SIAM gray matter voxels were fully relabeled. We observed that both SIAM and SynthSeg+ misclassified cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) voxels within the ventricular region as hippocampus or amygdala in a subset of participants. We corrected these segmentation errors using the following algorithm: For each participant, we derived a participant-specific intensity threshold by computing the equal-posterior boundary between two fitted Gaussian distributions, built from local reference populations sampled within a 15 mm neighborhood of the hippocampus–amygdala mask: ventricular and sulcal CSF labels served as the CSF reference, and cortical gray matter labels from the same neighborhood served as the gray matter reference. Among hippocampus–amygdala voxels whose T1w intensity fell on the CSF side of this threshold, we relabeled only those forming a spatially contiguous region connected to pre-existing CSF labels, ensuring that isolated low-intensity voxels not physically continuous with a CSF compartment were left unchanged. We visually inspected all segmentations to confirm the validity of the correction. We implemented both algorithms in custom Python code, available in the Snakemake workflow (see Data and code availability). We used the resulting hybrid segmentation (SIAM gray matter relabeled with the SynthSeg+ cortical parcellation and corrected for hippocampus–amygdala CSF misclassification) for all whole-brain analyses. 

### Quantitative MRI maps
We computed µFA and Ki from the preprocessed dMRI data using the function `nii2uFA_fwe` in the MatMRI package (version 0.1.17) [@baron_matmri_2026;@arezza_estimation_2023]. We computed FA and MD from the preprocessed linear tensor encoding images using the `nii2kurt` function in MatMRI with the default spatial regularization parameter of 0.5 [@hamilton_robust_2024]. We computed the MTsat map using the "qi mtsat" binary in the QUIT package (version 3.4) [@wood_quit_2018]. We then registered all maps to the T1w space using the image transformations calculated in the previous section.

## Analyses
### Whole brain
We examined eight regions of interest (ROIs) that are differentially affected in AD, FTD, and LBD: the frontal, parietal, temporal, cingulate, occipital and insular cortices, the hippocampus and the amygdala. In AD, the hippocampus, amygdala and temporal cortex are affected first, with later involvement of the parietal and posterior cingulate cortices [@planche_structural_2022;@whitwell_focal_2007]. In FTD, the frontal, insular, anterior cingulate and anterior temporal cortices and the amygdala are characteristically affected, with parietal involvement in corticobasal syndrome [@rascovsky_sensitivity_2011;@gorno-tempini_classification_2011;@planche_anatomical_2023;@finger_frontotemporal_2016]. In LBD, the medial temporal lobe is relatively preserved compared with AD, whereas the occipital, parietal, frontal and insular cortices are affected [@mckeith_diagnosis_2017;@whitwell_focal_2007;@conti_neuroanatomical_2025].

We combined cortical parcellation labels from the hybrid segmentation into these eight ROIs (details in the Snakemake workflow; see Data and code availability). We normalized the volume of each ROI by each participant’s total intracranial volume, which we approximated as the combined volume of all SynthSeg+ labels, and expressed it as a percentage. We then averaged each metric within each ROI across both hemispheres.

As an exploratory analysis, to obtain a high-level quantitative marker of the difference between participants with dementia and HC, we calculated the Mahalanobis distance (MhD) [@mahalanobis_reprint_2018] for each participant and ROI. The MhD measures the multivariate distance of an individual from a distribution while accounting for the covariance between features [@tremblay_mvcomp_2024]. Here, for each ROI, we calculated the MhD of each participant (including HC) from the distribution of the control group, using the six metrics in the study (FA, MD, µFA, Ki, MTsat and normalized volume) jointly.

### Hippocampus
Given the high prevalence of hippocampal involvement in neurodegenerative dementia, we performed a more detailed analysis of the hippocampus by characterizing measurements at the subfield level [@karat_diffusion_2024]. We used HippUnfold (version 2.2.0) [@dekraker_automated_2022;@dekraker_surface-intrinsic_2026] with the model trained on data from people with AD (`ADNI_T1w_v1`) [@karat_hippocampal_2025] for hippocampal surface generation and subfield delineation. We used the T1w modality with the default parameters. We manually inspected all hippocampal segmentations and surfaces for quality assessment, which resulted in the exclusion of seven participants (2 AD, 4 FTD and 1 LBD) due to artifacts in the hippocampal subfield segmentations. We excluded these participants from both the whole brain and hippocampus analyses, so that all analyses used the same sample.

We then sampled all T1w-registered metric maps onto the hippocampal surface vertices with Connectome Workbench (version 2.1.0, https://github.com/Washington-University/workbench). Finally, we extracted the data of the vertices labeled as subiculum, cornu ammonis fields 1–4 (CA1–CA4) and dentate gyrus (DG) in both hemispheres. HippUnfold also provided an estimate of the subfield volumes for each hemisphere, which we normalized by the same total intracranial volume estimate as the whole-brain ROIs and expressed as percentages, and surface-based metrics (thickness, gyrification, and curvature).

## Statistical analysis
For the whole brain analyses, we performed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for each ROI-metric combination to compare groups while controlling for age and sex. We checked the homogeneity of regression slopes, normality of residuals, and homogeneity of variance to confirm the validity of the ANCOVA model. When the homogeneity of regression slopes assumption was not met, we fitted a model that included the interaction between group and the violating covariate. We visually inspected violations of residual normality using Q-Q plots.

Each model compared the four diagnostic groups for one metric and ROI. We used partial omega squared ($\omega^2_p$) as the primary effect size measure, as it provides a bias-corrected estimate of variance explained, which is preferable to partial eta squared ($\eta^2_p$) for limited sample sizes [@kroes_demystifying_2025]. Finally, we corrected for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini–Yekutieli false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (α = 0.05), which controls the FDR under arbitrary dependence between tests, as expected for correlated metrics and ROIs.

We applied the same ANCOVA model, controlling for age and sex, to the MhD of each ROI. The MhD is non-negative and right-skewed: for multivariate normal data, the squared MhD follows a χ{sup}`2` distribution. In addition, participants with dementia are expected to have larger and more variable distances than HC. We therefore log-transformed the MhD before the ANCOVA to reduce skewness and stabilize its variance across groups, and checked the ANCOVA assumptions on the transformed values.

For ROI-metric combinations that survived FDR correction (p<0.05), we performed exploratory post-hoc pairwise group comparisons with age- and sex-adjusted estimated marginal means derived from the ANCOVA models. We treated the six pairwise contrasts (AD-HC, AD-FTD, ...) as the comparison family and applied the Holm correction within it. We reported partial Hedges’ g (g’) as the primary effect size measure, as it provides a bias-corrected version of partial Cohen’s d that is preferable for limited sample sizes.

For the hippocampal subfield analysis, we performed ANCOVAs controlling for age, sex and hemisphere for each metric and subfield combination. We included the surface-based metrics provided by HippUnfold in the ANCOVA analysis. Finally, we calculated $\omega^2_p$ for effect size measurement and applied the same FDR correction for multiple comparisons. 

We performed the analyses in Python (version 3.12.15) with pandas (3.0.6), NumPy (2.5.3), SciPy (1.18.1) and statsmodels (0.14.6), and in R (version 4.5.3), called through rpy2 (3.6.8), using car (3.1-5) for the ANCOVAs and assumption tests, effectsize (1.0.3) for the effect sizes, and marginaleffects (0.32.0) for the post-hoc comparisons. We created the figures with plotly (6.9.0), seaborn (0.13.2) and matplotlib (3.11.2), and the demographics table with tableone (0.9.6).

Group effects that survived FDR correction were found broadly throughout the brain for MD ([](#table_ancovaResults_significant)), where the largest effect sizes ($\omega^2_p$) were observed in cortical ROIs: temporal [F(3,44)=13.71, p=0.0004, $\omega^2_p$=0.46], frontal [F(3,41)=9.92, p=0.0035, $\omega^2_p$=0.35], cingulate [F(3,41)=8.30, p=0.0085, $\omega^2_p$=0.34] and insula [F(3,44)=7.89, p=0.0091, $\omega^2_p$=0.32], while µFA had the largest effect in the hippocampus [F(3,44)=7.47, p=0.0102, $\omega^2_p$=0.34]. MTsat had a large effect in the amygdala [F(3,41)=6.24, p=0.0374, $\omega^2_p$=0.36] and hippocampus [F(3,41)=5.90, p=0.0316, $\omega^2_p$=0.34]. The assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes was not met for 14 ROI-metric combinations (13 with a group × age and one with a group × sex interaction, p < 0.05), indicating differential covariate-metric relationships across groups. We therefore refit these models with the interaction term and evaluated only the group effect. Five of the combinations that survived FDR correction came from refitted models (Cingulate-MD, Frontal-MD, Frontal-µFA, Temporal-µFA and Temporal-Ki). Ki showed a group effect only in the temporal cortex, from the refitted model [F(3,41)=6.46, p=0.0262, $\omega^2_p$=0.25]. Supplementary [](#table_ancova_violations) lists all combinations that did not meet the ANCOVA assumptions before FDR correction.

# Discussion
This study examined the ability of advanced diffusion and magnetization transfer metrics to stratify different types of dementia. We focused on eight gray matter regions differentially affected across AD, FTD, and LBD, including the hippocampus (a key region in AD that is also affected in FTD), using state-of-the-art segmentation tools. The b-tensor encoding metrics used here (µFA, Ki) complement standard DTI metrics, as µFA is insensitive to orientation dispersion and Ki is sensitive to tissue heterogeneity. µFA is thought to be sensitive to neurite density [@lampinen_probing_2023], and recent work has shown that it is relatively insensitive to myelination changes in mice [@rahman_microstructural_2025]. In contrast, MTsat is highly correlated with myelin content [@helms_high-resolution_2008;@seiberlich_wood_chapter_2020]. µFA and MTsat thus provide complementary measures of axon integrity and myelination. Furthermore, Ki measures the variance of compartment diffusivities, which is thought to correlate with tissue heterogeneity. Combining these metrics with the MhD provides a more comprehensive quantification of microstructural neurodegeneration across dementias, and could help disentangle the different sources of microstructural change in dementia.

We explored the MhD as a tool to capture the overall dementia-related changes in the multidimensional metric space. All dementia groups showed higher MhD than HC. The frontal and temporal cortices showed distinct MhD profiles for each dementia group, with LBD most similar to HC, followed by FTD, and then AD (highest MhD). These metrics are expected to change with normal aging [@chen_optimization_2024;@kim_white_2026;@kamiya_brain_2020], which is in line with the overall trend in the MhD space for the HC and dementia groups. These results encourage further multivariate analyses of this metric space across dementias. Machine learning algorithms, which excel at handling multivariate spaces, could capture finer details of this space to further stratify dementia; we did not pursue this approach because these algorithms require large amounts of training data, which remain scarce.

MD had the largest effect sizes in the temporal, frontal, cingulate and insular cortices, while µFA had the largest effect in the hippocampus, and MTsat in both the hippocampus and amygdala. Increased cortical MD has been reported in AD, with changes that are detectable independently of cortical thinning [@montal_cortical_2018;@spotorno_diffusion_2024]. These changes reflect the loss of microstructure in these regions and align with the expected neurodegeneration of the hippocampus in AD and of the frontal and temporal cortices in FTD.

The metrics clearly separated AD and FTD from HC, and AD from LBD, but separated LBD from HC and AD from FTD poorly. The LBD group showed higher MhD than HC but barely differed from HC in the post-hoc analysis (only parietal MD). However, the LBD group had a narrower age distribution than the other groups, which may affect the age adjustment in our models. The low separability between LBD and HC is consistent with the limited cortical atrophy reported in dementia with Lewy bodies [@whitwell_focal_2007].

The AD group showed the most severe neurodegeneration for most ROIs in this cohort. This was also visible in the MhD, with the AD group having the largest distances from HC, and in the post-hoc analysis, where AD had the largest effect sizes. Overall, microstructural integrity followed a consistent order across groups, from highest to lowest: HC > LBD >> FTD > AD. Our findings further suggest that the temporal cortex is a strong candidate to capture this gradient. Interestingly, we observed the same ordering in the participants’ MoCA scores ([](#tbl_demographics)), suggesting the microstructural changes measured by these metrics may reflect overall cognitive decline.

We also observed changes in MTsat, suggesting demyelination in several ROIs. This aligns with recent reports that MTsat of the hippocampus decreases with age in people with AD [@wearn_quantitative_2025]. Further research is required to determine how demyelination differs across dementias. Advanced techniques such as inhomogeneous magnetization transfer [@varma_magnetization_2015] improve myelin specificity and are a viable option for future studies. Ki was higher in the dementia groups, but with smaller effect sizes than MD and µFA, and only in the temporal cortex. The high within-group variance of Ki may partly explain its lower sensitivity. Recent research suggests that Ki could be affected by multiple sub-kurtosis sources [@henriques_evidence_2021;@novello_vivo_2022], such as exchange and diffusion time dependence, which were not accounted for in this study.

Interestingly, µFA decreased in all subfields, which suggests a broad loss of axons. Given the well-characterized cell loss of the subiculum and CA1 in AD [@ashford_diffusion_2011;@karat_diffusion_2024], this may suggest that axons that project from upstream regions of the trisynaptic circuit (e.g., DG and CA3) are compromised when their targets in CA1 and the subiculum are lost. Hippocampal µFA reductions have also been reported in temporal lobe epilepsy, where they were localized to the CA4/DG region affected by hippocampal sclerosis and outperformed volume in detecting pathology [@arezza_hippocampal_2024]. Although the underlying pathology differs from that of neurodegenerative dementia, this supports µFA as a sensitive marker of hippocampal neuronal loss that complements volumetry. MD is sensitive to these changes, but the underlying microstructural properties remain unclear [@karat_diffusion_2024], and µFA could help disentangle them as a complementary measure.

µFA and MTsat both showed decreasing trends with age in the hippocampus (Supplementary [](#fig_hippocampus_ageMetrics)) and appeared to decline more steeply in participants with dementia, particularly AD, which may suggest decreasing axonal content during normal aging that is accelerated with dementia. Others have reported decreasing µFA with healthy aging [@kamiya_brain_2020;@andersen_disentangling_2020], and a similar pattern for MTsat [@wearn_quantitative_2025]. While the natural progression of aging affects these metrics, dementia subtypes may accelerate the measured degenerative process. This study treated age as a covariate, but further research with larger numbers of participants is required to determine whether and how aging interacts with or drives structural neurodegeneration in dementia.

Other studies have used biophysical models [@novikov_modeling_2018] to characterize these dementias. Some studies rely on the neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) model [@zhang_noddi_2012] and report the sensitivity of its metrics to AD and FTD [@veale_loss_2021;@lv_analysis_2025;@pisano_frontotemporal_2025]. Other modeling approaches, such as the recently developed soma and neurite density imaging (SANDI) model [@palombo_sandi_2020], are tailored for gray matter cellular microstructure. Recent studies with high-gradient systems have reported SANDI metrics in aging brain structures [@lee_age-related_2024;@ma_age-related_2026], providing evidence that these metrics could be sensitive to neurodegenerative microstructural changes in AD and FTD. However, biophysical models rely on tissue assumptions that may need additional validation in these dementia types [@jelescu_challenges_2020]. Characterization of AD, FTD, and LBD with advanced representation metrics, including µFA, which had considerable effect sizes in this study, could provide complementary information to further improve biophysical modeling aimed at diagnosing dementia. This is especially relevant for b-tensor encoding acquisitions, which could relax model assumptions and yield more specific microstructural metrics [@lampinen_towards_2020;@gyori_potential_2021].

Multiple limitations should be considered. First, the analysis was performed on a small dataset, which may limit generalizability beyond our cohort and increases the chance of false negatives due to low statistical power. Nevertheless, the main effects were large enough to be detected even in this small cohort. Second, the LBD group was not properly age-matched to the other groups, and changes produced by healthy aging could be a confounding factor in its analysis when compared to the HC group [@ma_age-related_2026]. This could partially explain why, when correcting for age, this group barely differed from HC (only parietal MD). Third, the segmentation tools could not delineate the ROIs properly for some participants, which reduced our sample and may have biased our results toward participants with less severe neurodegeneration. This may explain why hippocampal volume did not differ between groups after FDR correction. SIAM, SynthSeg+, and HippUnfold (default model) use machine learning algorithms that were trained on data from healthy individuals and still struggle in brains with ongoing neurodegeneration. Hippocampal segmentations improved when we used HippUnfold with the AD model [@karat_hippocampal_2025], suggesting that these tools could improve as more high-resolution dementia datasets become available. Even in these participants with less severe volume loss, we observed large reductions in µFA in the hippocampus. Fourth, the voxel size of the MRI images is relatively large for microstructural studies, especially for hippocampal subfields, and partial volume effects may be affecting the current study. Free water elimination techniques for b-tensor encoding [@arezza_estimation_2023] could reduce partial volume effects from the ventricles. However, they require additional encoding shells at low b-values which were not acquired in this study. Fifth, our ROIs combined lobes across both hemispheres, which may average out sub-regional and lateralized patterns, especially for FTD subgroups, which we were not able to explore given our sample size. Finally, recent research has shown that b-tensor-derived metrics could be biased by diffusion time dependence arising from the spectral content of the waveforms [@lasic_spectral_2025;@szczepankiewicz_restriction-weighted_2026]. The b-tensor acquisition scheme used here is susceptible to this effect, and the metrics derived could present this bias. Future b-tensor encoding dementia studies should account for this bias as time dependence effects are present in gray matter regions [@olesen_diffusion_2022;@jelescu_neurite_2022].

These results also motivate several avenues for future work. The relationship between the b-tensor encoding metrics (µFA and Ki) and the hallmark misfolded proteins of each dementia type, including TDP-43 and tau in FTD, amyloid-β in AD and α-synuclein in LBD, is currently unknown and should be examined. Recent research has shown links between standard dMRI metrics and α-synuclein [@mak_alpha_synuclein_2026] and between MTsat and tau and amyloid-β deposits [@moallemian_multimodal_2023;@wearn_quantitative_2025], which could benefit from the additional information contained in b-tensor encoding metrics as presented in this work. Future studies with larger samples should also examine differences in µFA and Ki outcomes per FTD subtype, particularly the behavioral and semantic variants, the most common subtypes, as they affect distinctly different brain regions [@finger_frontotemporal_2016;@gordon_advances_2016]. Overall, µFA provided information complementary to the widely used standard DTI and volumetric metrics, which could improve our understanding of the spatiotemporal progression of neurodegenerative dementia.





# Conclusion
In this study, we showed how advanced dMRI metrics and MTsat varied across brain regions in AD, FTD, and LBD compared with healthy controls. Probing the multivariate space of these metrics with MhD showed promise for differentiating between dementia types. We identified the combinations of metrics and ROIs that best differentiated the groups. In the hippocampus, µFA patterns varied across dementia types, making it a potential marker of hippocampal degeneration that could complement standard metrics for dementia stratification.
